# NCS fundamentals

## What each parameter measures
Distal latency is the time from stimulation to the onset (motor) or peak (sensory) of the response. It reflects conduction in the fastest fibres across the distal segment plus, for motor studies, neuromuscular transmission. Conduction velocity is computed from the distance between two stimulation sites divided by the latency difference, and it reflects the speed of the fastest myelinated fibres. Both latency and velocity depend mainly on myelin integrity.

Amplitude reflects the number of axons (and, for motor studies, muscle fibres) that conduct. A compound muscle action potential (CMAP) is measured in millivolts; a sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) in microvolts. Low amplitude with preserved velocity points to axonal loss.

## Axonal versus demyelinating physiology
In axonal loss, amplitudes fall while velocities stay normal or are only mildly slowed, because the surviving fastest fibres still conduct normally. A useful convention is that velocity above roughly 75% of the lower limit of normal is still compatible with pure axonal loss. Slowing below about 75% of the lower limit, or distal latency prolonged beyond about 130% of the upper limit, implies primary demyelination. Conduction block (a large drop in amplitude between distal and proximal stimulation) and temporal dispersion are further demyelinating features.

## Physiological and technical variables
Cooling slows conduction: velocity falls by roughly 1.5-2.5 m/s and distal latency rises by roughly 0.2 ms for every 1 C drop in limb temperature. Upper-limb skin temperature should be kept at or above 32 C, or values corrected. Velocities decrease slightly with ageing after the fourth decade, and amplitudes, particularly SNAPs, decline with age. Taller subjects have longer F-wave latencies. Electrode placement, distance measurement error and supramaximal stimulation all affect results.
